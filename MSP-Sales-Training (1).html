What is IT?

IT stands for Information Technology — everything that keeps a business running from a technology standpoint. This includes computers, email, internet, networks, security, data, and the systems people use every day to do their jobs.

A simple way to think about IT is in four parts: the people using it, the devices they use, the network that connects everything, and the security that protects it.

Break-Fix vs. Managed IT

Traditionally, many businesses handled IT reactively — something breaks, someone fixes it. This is called break-fix IT. The problem is that issues are only addressed after they happen, leading to downtime, frustration, and security risks.

Managed IT Services take a different approach. Instead of waiting for issues, systems are proactively monitored, updated, and supported on a continuous basis — meaning fewer problems, less downtime, and a more secure environment.

What is an MSP?

MSP stands for Managed Service Provider — a company responsible for managing and supporting a business's IT environment on an ongoing basis. Think of it as having a dedicated team working behind the scenes to keep everything running smoothly, rather than only stepping in when something goes wrong.

What is a user?

A user is anyone who logs into a system, email, or device — employees, managers, and sometimes vendors or partners with access to company systems. Every user has credentials (typically a username and password) to access the tools they need.

Why identity matters

Identity refers to who someone is in a digital environment and what they are allowed to access. Managing identity means making sure the right people have access to the right systems — and just as importantly, that they don't have access to things they shouldn't.

One of the biggest risks in IT today is compromised user accounts. For example, a phishing email tricks someone into entering their password on a fake website — an attacker who gains that access can reach email, files, and other systems.

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

MFA adds an extra layer of protection by requiring a second step at login — such as a code sent to a phone or an authentication app. Even if a password is compromised, MFA makes it much harder for an attacker to gain access.

User lifecycle management

User management also includes creating accounts for new employees, removing access when someone leaves, and updating permissions as roles change. If an employee leaves and their account isn't disabled, that creates a major security risk.

What is an endpoint?

An endpoint is any device that connects to a network — laptops, desktops, and mobile devices. These are where most work gets done, but they are also one of the most common entry points for security threats.

Device health monitoring

Endpoint management includes monitoring the health of devices to ensure they're running properly and identifying issues before they become major problems. If a device starts slowing down or showing signs of failure, it can be addressed early before it impacts the user.

Patching

Patching means regularly installing updates for the operating system and software. These updates fix security vulnerabilities and improve performance. If devices aren't kept up to date, they become easy targets for cyber threats.

If a known vulnerability exists and a device hasn't been patched, attackers can take advantage of that weakness. A single compromised device can impact not just one user — but potentially the entire business.

Endpoint security tools

Endpoint management also includes security tools that monitor activity on devices and look for signs of suspicious behavior. If something unusual is detected, it can be investigated and addressed before it becomes a larger issue.

What is the network?

The network is what connects everything together — internet connections, WiFi, firewalls, switches, access points, and the systems that allow devices and applications to communicate. Every computer, phone, printer, and cloud application depends on the network functioning properly.

Internet connectivity

Most businesses today rely heavily on cloud applications and online systems. If the internet goes down, employees may lose access to email, shared files, cloud software, phone systems, and critical business applications. Even short outages create downtime and lost productivity.

WiFi & access points

WiFi needs to be properly designed, securely configured, monitored for performance, and capable of supporting all connected users and devices. An access point is the device that broadcasts the wireless signal throughout the building — most businesses have multiple access points for full coverage.

If access points are poorly placed or overloaded, employees may experience dropped connections, slow speeds, poor call quality, or unreliable application performance.

Firewalls

A firewall acts as a security gatekeeper between the internal network and the internet — monitoring and controlling what traffic is allowed in and out. Without a properly configured firewall, businesses are much more exposed to unauthorized access, malicious traffic, and external attacks.

Switches

Switches physically connect devices together inside the network. Computers, printers, phones, servers, and access points often connect back to network switches. While users may never see them directly, switches play a major role in keeping communication flowing throughout the environment.

Network management

Network management covers monitoring traffic, identifying outages or slowdowns, maintaining security settings, updating firmware, and ensuring systems operate efficiently as the business grows. A well-managed network reduces downtime, improves productivity, supports remote work, and strengthens overall cybersecurity.

What is cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity is the process of protecting systems, devices, users, and data from threats — including phishing emails, ransomware, malicious websites, stolen passwords, unauthorized access, and social engineering attacks.

Today, almost every business is a target. Small and mid-sized businesses are attacked every day because attackers know they often have fewer resources and less protection in place.

Common threats

Phishing is when someone receives an email or message designed to look legitimate, tricking them into clicking a malicious link, downloading malware, entering their password, or sending sensitive information.

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts files and systems, making them inaccessible until a ransom payment is made. It can spread quickly across a network and lead to downtime, lost revenue, and significant recovery costs.

Modern cybersecurity approach

Traditional antivirus alone isn't enough anymore. Modern threats are designed to bypass it. Today's approach is built around five pillars:

  • Prevention — reducing the attack surface before threats occur
  • Monitoring — continuous visibility into system activity
  • Detection — identifying suspicious behavior quickly
  • Response — containing and neutralizing threats fast
  • Recovery — restoring systems after an incident

Huntress — our cybersecurity layer

We use Huntress as an additional layer of cybersecurity protection. Huntress continuously monitors endpoints and systems for signs of malicious activity. When something suspicious is detected, a real human security team reviews it — not automation alone — improving accuracy and response speed.

If a threat is confirmed, the affected device can be isolated (quarantined) almost immediately to prevent it from spreading across the network.

It's also about people

Many cyber incidents begin with simple human mistakes — clicking a bad link, reusing passwords, or responding to fraudulent emails. Education and awareness are critical parts of a strong cybersecurity strategy. Cybersecurity is not just one tool — it's a layered approach designed to reduce risk across the entire environment.

Your role

The goal of this section is not to turn you into a technical expert. Your role is much simpler: recognize opportunities, ask a few good questions, and comfortably open the door to a Managed IT conversation. You are not expected to troubleshoot problems or provide technical solutions on the spot.

Opportunities are everywhere

Almost every business depends heavily on technology. Customers will often bring up frustrations naturally — slow computers, unreliable WiFi, remote employees, cybersecurity concerns, downtime, or simply "things never seem to work right." All of those can indicate an opportunity.

Great opening questions

  • "What are you guys doing for IT today?" — casual, non-threatening, and easy to answer
  • "Who handles your computers and cybersecurity today?"
  • "Do you have internal IT, or do you work with an outside IT company?"

Signals to listen for

  • "Our IT guy is overloaded" / "Support takes forever"
  • "We've had some security scares" / "We've had phishing problems"
  • "Our systems are always down"
  • "We've grown a lot recently" / "We're adding new locations"
  • "We don't really know if we're protected"

Environmental changes also signal opportunity: company growth, remote employees, compliance requirements, new software rollouts, aging infrastructure, or leadership concerns around cybersecurity.

Co-managed IT

Many businesses use co-managed IT — keeping internal IT staff but partnering with an MSP for cybersecurity, endpoint monitoring, patching, after-hours support, or compliance. Don't approach these conversations as "we want to replace your IT." Focus on learning about their environment and identifying where additional support may help.

How to transition the conversation

  • "That's actually something our Managed IT team helps businesses with quite a bit. Would you be open to a quick conversation with them?"
  • "We've been helping a lot of businesses simplify and secure their IT. I'd love to set up a quick intro with our Managed IT team."
